The pronoun trap: "she hulk"

The same thing happens to pronouns. A tokenizer that splits on hyphens turns every She-Hulk into the pronoun "she" followed by the word "hulk". Ask what usually comes after "she" in these pages, and "hulk" is the second answer.

      How we did it, and what it doesn't show

      • Tokenizer. We counted with scikit-learn's CountVectorizer, the tool from exercise 5.7. Like spaCy, it splits "Spider-Man" into "spider" and "man". A plain split() on spaces would keep "Spider-Man" whole and avoid this particular trap.
      • What counts as a name. A mention is a name when it is glued to a hyphen (X-Men, She-Hulk) or capitalised right after another capitalised word (Iron Man, Moon Girl). Lower-case uses are real words. That rule also sweeps in a few titles, such as "Sexiest Women", which is why the bucket says "names or titles".
      • What we checked by hand. We read samples of both kinds in context (the panel above) before trusting the rule.
      • Limitation. The real-word counts are small: 224 "man" against 121 "woman". The corrected ratio of 1.9 to 1 is a rough figure, and a word like "man" says nothing about which characters are men. This is a warning about measurement, not a census of Marvel's gender balance.
      • What didn't hold up. We also tested whether women's pages are longer (they are, but women in this list are more linked, and the gap disappears once you account for links) and whether women's pages talk more about death and resurrection (per word, they don't).
