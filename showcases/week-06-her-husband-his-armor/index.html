      The hero exam

      Pick any two of the 303 heroes. We read their own page and list the words that follow “her” or “his”, whichever the page uses more, next to the average for that pronoun.

      How we did it, with Week 6 tools

      lean(w) = log₂ P(w | her) / P(w | his) = PMI(her, w) − PMI(his, w)

      • Context window of 1. For each “her” and “his” we keep the single next word, the narrowest window from exercise 6.6. Narrow windows capture how a word is used grammatically, which is what we want here.
      • PMI to choose the words. A word’s lean is its PMI with “her” minus its PMI with “his”, with add-one smoothing so words that never follow one side still get a score. The charts show the words leaning hardest each way among words used often enough; the explorer’s sliders change both cut-offs.
      • Rates, not counts. “His” is × as common as “her”, so bars show uses per 1,000 words after each pronoun. The numbers next to the bars are raw counts.
      • Two groups of text, as in Scattertext. The “her” contexts and the “his” contexts are the two groups being compared. The themes (family, body, gear, mind, career) are our own labels, like topic labels in exercise 6.4.
      • What we checked by hand. We read the sentences behind every word in the charts. That is how we found that “her telepathy” sits on a handful of pages, that “his healing” is almost always a healing factor, and that “comic” and “full” are artefacts of Wikipedia’s style.
      • Limitations. Dropping function words removes most cases where “her” is an object (“saved her from”), but not all. Adjectives count too (“her telepathic”). In the hero exam, “his” on a man’s page sometimes refers to another man. And this is how Wikipedia editors summarise the comics, not the comics themselves.
