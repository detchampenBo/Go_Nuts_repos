What we asked

Who sits in the middle of Marvel?

The network has a standard answer: the hero with the most links. For the 303 pages, that's , linked with of them. We wanted the text's answer. Week 7 is about putting documents into a vector space and asking who is near whom: nearest neighbours, dense retrieval, RAG. So we asked the space directly. If every page names the pages it reads most like, whose name comes up most?

The representation and the measurement

Pages to vectors, vectors to votes.

  • Pages to vectors. Each page, up to its references, is cut into ~120-word chunks ( in all). Each chunk is embedded with all-MiniLM-L6-v2, and a page's vector is the normalised mean of its chunks.
  • Votes. Each page picks the 10 other pages with the highest cosine similarity. A page's score is how many pages picked it, its k-occurrence. With 303 pages each casting 10 votes, the average is exactly 10.
  • The network. The week-1 hyperlinks, undirected: a page's degree is the number of pages it shares a link with ( linked pairs in total).
  • The control. The same vote on 303 random unit vectors with the same 384 dimensions. If the text weren't doing anything special, its votes would spread like these.
  • The fix. The same vote after centring: subtracting the average page vector from every page before cosine, a standard way to reduce hubs in nearest-neighbour search.

What surprised us

The centre is a hero who is many heroes.

You'd expect the text's centre to be a famous hero with a long page. It's Captain Universe, a minor character with links. Its page explains why it fits the middle so well:

Rather than a character with a single identity, it is a persona that has merged with several hosts during its publication history. … Although the Uni-Power typically empowers normal, non-super-powered humans, it has in the past empowered Doctor Strange, Spider-Man, Commander Arcturus Rann of the Micronauts, a toddler, and a dog, among others.Wikipedia, "Captain Universe" (course snapshot, 26 August 2026)

The general rule behind it: the pages that get picked most are the pages most like the average page (Spearman ρ = between picks and cosine to the mean vector). In a high-dimensional space, a point near the middle is close to everything, so it lands in everyone's top 10. This is called hubness. A page about a power that has possessed many different heroes is about as close to an average Marvel page as text gets. Captain Universe's page also names of the other heroes, against a median of .

The losers are just as telling. All pages nobody picks are short and specific: of them are shorter than the median page of words. Baymax, Box and Typeface are among them.

Why it matters for week 7

A hub gets retrieved for everything.

Hubness isn't only a curiosity on a map. In dense retrieval, the first step of RAG, a hub page is returned for questions it has nothing to do with. We used each of the chunks as a query against the other pages. Captain Universe came back in the top 5 for of them, from different pages. The median page came back times.

Centring the vectors is the cheap fix, and it works here. Captain Universe's picks fall from to , nobody is left unpicked, and its retrievals drop from to . Retrieval barely suffers: a chunk finds its own page in the top 5 of the time before centring and after.

One limitation

"Average" depends on who's in the room.

A hub is near the middle of this corpus: 303 pages from one Wikipedia category, embedded by one small encoder and averaged over chunks. Mean pooling itself pulls long, varied pages toward the middle (picks rise with page length, ρ = ). A different encoder, a different pooling, or a corpus with Captain America and Thor in it (neither has a page here) could crown someone else. The finding we'd defend is the shape: a few pages are near everyone and many are near no one, and the text's centre is not the network's centre.
